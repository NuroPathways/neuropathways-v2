# 16. Starter Template: Elopement / Running Away

Behavior name:

**Elopement / Running Away**

Parent-friendly description:

The child tries to leave the area, run away, bolt from an activity, or move toward an unsafe place.

What may be happening:

The child may be trying to escape a demand, reach something preferred, avoid overwhelm, or respond to sensory overload.

What to do right now:

1. Stay calm and move between the child and unsafe areas if possible.
2. Reduce verbal demands.
3. Use a short safety phrase such as “Stop” or “Come back.”
4. Guide the child to a safer area.
5. Once safe, offer a simple choice or calming option.

What to avoid:

- yelling from far away
- chasing in a way that turns it into a game
- giving long explanations during the moment
- adding new demands while the child is still unsafe

Prevention:

- use transition warnings
- use visual cues
- identify high-risk locations
- prepare before leaving the house
- reinforce staying near caregiver

Replacement skill:

Teach the child to ask for a break, hold a safety item, stop at a boundary, or use a help signal.

Reinforcement idea:

Praise and reward staying close, stopping when called, or returning safely.

When to seek help:

If the child moves toward traffic, water, exits, parking lots, or other danger, seek immediate emergency support.

What to track:

- where it happened
- what happened right before
- where the child tried to go
- what helped them return
- safety risk level

---

# 17. Starter Template: Self-Injury

Behavior name:

**Self-Injury**

Parent-friendly description:

The child hurts themselves or attempts to hurt themselves, such as hitting their head, biting, scratching, or other unsafe actions.

What may be happening:

The child may be overwhelmed, frustrated, unable to communicate, in pain, seeking escape, or needing sensory input.

What to do right now:

1. Reduce demands immediately.
2. Move dangerous objects away.
3. Use a calm, low voice.
4. Block only if needed for safety.
5. Offer a safer replacement if one is known.
6. Move toward a calm, low-stimulation space if possible.

What to avoid:

- yelling
- punishment during escalation
- physically restraining unless safety requires it
- asking too many questions during the moment
- ignoring serious injury risk

Prevention:

- watch for early warning signs
- reduce known triggers
- offer breaks before escalation
- use communication supports
- check for pain, hunger, tiredness, or sensory overload

Replacement skill:

Ask for break, use safe sensory item, squeeze pillow, use communication card, ask for help.

Reinforcement idea:

Reinforce safe communication, calm body, and using replacement skills.

When to seek help:

If injury is serious, repeated, escalating, or medically concerning, contact emergency services or the child’s clinician.

What to track:

- type of self-injury
- intensity
- duration
- trigger
- injury risk
- what reduced the behavior

---

# 18. Starter Template: Aggression

Behavior name:

**Aggression**

Parent-friendly description:

The child hits, kicks, bites, pushes, throws items at people, or uses unsafe physical behavior toward others.

What may be happening:

The child may be overwhelmed, frustrated, trying to escape a demand, seeking attention, denied access to something, or unable to communicate needs.

What to do right now:

1. Create space and reduce demands.
2. Move other people or unsafe objects away.
3. Use a calm, brief instruction.
4. Avoid arguing.
5. Offer a safe alternative such as break, help, or space.
6. Reinforce the first calm or safe action.

What to avoid:

- arguing
- lecturing during escalation
- crowding the child
- sudden punishment
- raising your voice
- repeating the same demand rapidly

Prevention:

- identify common triggers
- use choices
- prepare for denied access
- teach requesting help or break
- reinforce calm communication early

Replacement skill:

Ask for help, ask for space, use words/gesture, request break, use calm-down tool.

Reinforcement idea:

Praise safe hands, calm body, asking for help, or moving away appropriately.

When to seek help:

If aggression causes injury, becomes severe, or cannot be safely managed, contact clinician or emergency support.

What to track:

- who was nearby
- what happened right before
- what the child wanted or avoided
- how long it lasted
- what helped

# 19. Starter Template: Meltdowns

Behavior name:

**Meltdowns / Emotional Dysregulation**

Parent-friendly description:

The child becomes highly overwhelmed, cries, screams, shuts down, drops to the floor, or cannot respond normally.

What may be happening:

The child may be overloaded, anxious, tired, frustrated, overstimulated, or unable to process demands.

What to do right now:

1. Reduce talking.
2. Lower lights/noise if possible.
3. Give space while maintaining safety.
4. Use calm body language.
5. Offer one simple calming option.
6. Wait until the child is calmer before problem solving.

What to avoid:

- asking many questions
- demanding eye contact
- forcing conversation
- threatening consequences during escalation
- trying to teach a lesson in the moment

Prevention:

- watch for early warning signs
- use predictable routines
- offer breaks
- reduce sensory overload
- prepare for difficult transitions

Replacement skill:

Use calm-down space, ask for break, use breathing/grounding tool, point to feelings chart, request help.

Reinforcement idea:

Reinforce using calming tools, recovering safely, or communicating needs.

When to seek help:

If the meltdown includes unsafe behavior, self-injury, aggression, or long duration beyond what is typical, contact clinician or emergency support if needed.

What to track:

- trigger
- early warning signs
- duration
- calming tools used
- recovery time
- safety concerns

---

# 20. Starter Template: Task Refusal

Behavior name:

**Task Refusal**

Parent-friendly description:

The child refuses, avoids, ignores, or escalates when asked to complete homework, chores, hygiene, therapy practice, or another non-preferred task.

What may be happening:

The task may feel too hard, too long, boring, overwhelming, or frustrating. The child may be trying to escape the demand.

What to do right now:

1. Break the task into one small step.
2. Use first-then language.
3. Offer two acceptable choices.
4. Reduce extra talking.
5. Praise the first attempt.
6. Give a short break if appropriate, then return to the task.

What to avoid:

- repeating the demand over and over
- turning it into an argument
- adding extra demands
- removing all rewards immediately
- starting with a large task

Prevention:

- make expectations clear
- use timers
- use visual checklist
- start with easier steps
- offer choices
- reinforce effort early

Replacement skill:

Ask for help, ask for break, choose first step, use coping strategy, complete small part of task.

Reinforcement idea:

Praise effort and task start, not only full completion.

When to seek help:

If refusal becomes unsafe, intense, or consistently prevents daily functioning, bring it to the clinician.

What to track:

- task type
- time of day
- demand difficulty
- refusal duration
- strategy used
- whether the child returned to task

---

# 21. Starter Template: Transition Difficulty

Behavior name:

**Transition Difficulty**

Parent-friendly description:

The child struggles when moving from one activity to another, especially from preferred to non-preferred activities.

What may be happening:

The child may need more predictability, more processing time, help ending a preferred activity, or support understanding what comes next.

What to do right now:

1. Give a calm transition warning.
2. Use a timer or visual cue.
3. Say what is ending and what is next.
4. Offer a small choice.
5. Use first-then language.
6. Reinforce any movement toward the next activity.

What to avoid:

- sudden transitions without warning
- grabbing items away without preparation
- long explanations during escalation
- arguing about the transition
- changing instructions repeatedly

Prevention:

- use visual schedules
- use countdowns
- practice transitions when calm
- create consistent routines
- give transition object if helpful

Replacement skill:

Use transition card, ask for one more minute, choose next step, walk with caregiver, use calm body.

Reinforcement idea:

Praise moving to the next step, stopping preferred activity safely, or using transition tools.

When to seek help:

If transition difficulty regularly becomes unsafe or prevents routines, ask clinician to build a specific transition plan.

What to track:

- activity ending
- next activity
- warning used
- reaction
- how long transition took
- what helped
